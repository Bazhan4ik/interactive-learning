Some quantities are fully described by a single number: a temperature of 21°, a mass of 3 kg. Others need a **direction** as well. Saying a car moves at 50 km/h is incomplete. Saying it moves at 50 km/h *heading north* describes its **velocity**.

A **vector** is a quantity with both a **magnitude** (size) and a **direction**. Plain numbers are called **scalars**.

### Drawing vectors

We draw a vector as an arrow. Its length shows the magnitude, and the way it points shows the direction.

Position doesn't matter: two arrows with the same length and the same direction are the *same* vector, wherever they are drawn. That freedom is what makes the next sections work.

### Notation

Vectors are usually written in bold, **v**, or with an arrow on top, v⃗. In two dimensions a vector is described by its **components**: how far it reaches along the x-axis and along the y-axis.

**v** = (3, 2)

means "3 units to the right and 2 units up".

### Magnitude

The magnitude of **v** is written |**v**|. The components form a right triangle with the arrow, so by Pythagoras:

|**v**| = √(x² + y²)

For **v** = (3, 2), that gives |**v**| = √(9 + 4) = √13 ≈ 3.61.

A vector with magnitude 1 is called a **unit vector**. It carries direction only, and is often used to say "which way" without saying "how much".
