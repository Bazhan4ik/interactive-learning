Multiplying a vector by a scalar $k$ **scales** it. Each component is multiplied by $k$:

$$
k\,(x, y) = (kx,\; ky)
$$

What happens depends on $k$:

- $k > 1$ stretches the vector
- $0 < k < 1$ shrinks it
- $k < 0$ flips it to point the opposite way
- $k = 0$ collapses it to the zero vector $\mathbf{0} = (0, 0)$

The result always stays on the same line through the origin as $\mathbf{v}$. Only the length changes, and possibly which way along the line it points. The new length is the old one times the size of $k$:

$$
|k\mathbf{v}| = |k|\,|\mathbf{v}|
$$

Move the slider to change $k$, and drag $\mathbf{v}$ to try other vectors. Notice that $(-1)\,\mathbf{v}$ is exactly the $-\mathbf{b}$ from the previous section.

Scaling and adding together let you build new vectors like $2\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$. With two vectors that don't lie on the same line, such **linear combinations** reach every point in the plane. That idea is at the heart of linear algebra.
